Is that terrace in the sun?

22 November 2026 · By AlephB · About Compromise

On a spring evening, which side of the street you sit on can matter more than which bar. One terrace still has an hour of sun; the one opposite went into shade at five. Compromise, the meeting-point app I build, has a filter for this. It keeps the terraces that will be in the sun when your group arrives and says until when. A second switch, for heatwaves, keeps the shaded ones instead. Here is how the phone works that out without a server, and where it still gets it wrong.

Where the sun is

The sun's position is pure astronomy and needs no data source. The app uses NOAA's solar position algorithm, the version from their spreadsheet, which gives the sun's elevation above the horizon to about a tenth of a degree. One more step of the same algorithm gives the azimuth, the sun's compass bearing. The shadow test needs those two angles and nothing else from the sky.

The tests check it against known figures: in Paris the sun stands at 64.5° at noon on the summer solstice and 17.7° at noon in winter, and it sets around 20:00 UTC in June. Sunrise and sunset follow the almanac rule, with the sun's centre 0.833° below the horizon to allow for refraction and the size of the disc. Polar night reads dark all day; midnight sun never does.

The first version of the filter used the sun alone. A terrace passed if the sun was more than 5° above the horizon when you would get there, because below 5° a street is in shadow whatever the buildings are like. It needed no data and was nearly useless, since every bar in a narrow street "passed" at five in the afternoon.

Buildings, and the heights nobody measured

To know whether a pavement is lit you need the outlines and heights of the buildings around it. Outlines are easy to find. Heights are not.

In France the source is IGN's BD TOPO, the national topographic database, published under the Licence Ouverte, which gives a height for most footprints. The first build fetched it live from IGN's public web service: a 600 m box around the Opéra in Paris came back as 448 buildings, 680 KB, in 0.24 seconds, and about 10% of them had no height. Some other cities publish their own footprints, sometimes from lidar. Everywhere else the buildings come from OpenStreetMap (© OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL), where a height is either a height tag or a number of floors.

Floors become metres at 3 m a floor, and that rule has a known bias. Checked against IGN's measured heights around the Palais Garnier, floors × 3 m came out about 2 m low on the Haussmann blocks, with their tall ground floors and the extra storey in the roof.

Then there are the buildings with no height at all, which in some cities are most of them. A survey of dense going-out districts in OpenStreetMap-only cities found a height on 0.4% of buildings in Seoul and 4.5% in Rome. For a long time every building without one was drawn at a flat 15 m. In Athens, where about 4% of buildings had a height or a floor count, that turned the low old centre into a canyon. The tagged buildings in Psyrri, Monastiraki and Plaka have a median height of 6 to 9 m, so the default shaded terraces that were really in the sun.

The rule now is local. A building with no height takes the median of the measured heights in its own tile (a cell of about 200 m, plus 200 m around it) once there are at least 10. Failing that, it takes the median of the wider block the build fetched it with, about 1.6 km across, and only then 15 m. To test it I held out a fifth of the measured heights, five times over, and estimated each one from the rest. The mean error fell in every city tested: Athens from 5.8 m to 4.3 m, Edinburgh from 5.1 m to 2.9 m, Paris from 5.0 m to 3.5 m. In Berlin and Tokyo the worst cases got worse, because a neighbourhood median falls further short of a tower than 15 m did. There is also a bias the test cannot see. Volunteer mappers tag landmarks and new blocks first, so the buildings with a height are not a random sample. The 6 m estimate for Monastiraki rests on 75 tagged footprints out of 1,154.

A guessed height is stored so it can always be told from a measured one: in quarter-metre steps such as 9.25, which no measured height takes, or as no value for the 15 m default. The shadow test uses a guess like any other height, since a typical local height errs both ways. The rooftop test, below, does not.

I also probed Overture Maps, which merges OpenStreetMap with Microsoft's machine-learned building heights. Where Microsoft has a height it is good: against IGN in Bobigny, the median error was 1.3 m. But it adds a height to only a quarter to a tenth of buildings in the best cities (Stuttgart, Washington, Copenhagen, Naples), almost none in Lisbon or Athens, and nothing in France that IGN lacks. It is still a probe and is not in the app.

Casting the shadow

From the terrace's point, the app walks toward the sun along its azimuth, one metre at a time, out to 150 m. At each step it checks whether it is inside a building footprint. If it is, and the building is taller than the sun's ray at that distance (its height beats the distance times the tangent of the sun's elevation), the terrace is in shadow. If the ray reaches 150 m without meeting anything tall enough, the terrace is in the sun.

The moment it judges is when the whole group has arrived: the departure plus the longest journey to that place. A terrace sunny when you leave home is no use if it is in shade by the time the last person sits down.

"Until when" reuses the same test. From the arrival, the app steps forward ten minutes at a time and stops at the first step where the answer flips, which gives a caption like "In the sun until 19:40". It looks at most four hours ahead. The shade filter does the same in reverse.

Where exactly the terrace is

A venue on a map is one point, and that point is often inside its own building. A probe in September found terrace points on the venue's own footprint for 149 of 150 terraces in Amsterdam, 40 of 43 in San Francisco and 210 of 314 in Toulouse. A ray that starts inside a building is blocked by it as soon as the sun is low, and seven covered cities showed no sunny terrace at any hour.

Such a point now moves 2 m past the nearest wall that opens onto open ground, usually the façade where the tables stand. When several footprints hold the point, it uses the largest, because some cities publish one outline per level of a tower, and 2 m past the small upper outline is still inside the podium. The rule has a known flaw: the nearest wall is not always the street wall, so a venue drawn at its back door is judged in its courtyard, and a corner venue may be judged on the wrong side.

Where a city publishes its terrace permits, as Paris does with more than 24,000 of them under ODbL, the test runs at the permit's point, on the tables. A permit is matched to a venue by name, with accents and punctuation stripped, within 30 m. An early version also took any permit right at the door, and in the first live test around the Opéra one bar inherited its neighbour's terrace that way. The door rule now applies only to permits with no name. Where a city publishes no permits, the app judges the sun side of the street, at the venue's own front, and the caption says so.

Rooftop bars get their own test. If the venue's name or its OpenStreetMap tags say it is on a roof, the point is judged at roof height, and only what rises above the roof can shade it. That needs a measured building height. On a guessed one the app does not invent a roof and judges the venue at street level.

Making it fast on a phone

The naive version is slow: 150 one-metre steps, about 900 buildings in a cell, and two dozen checks per venue for its caption, for every venue in the list. Before walking the ray, the app keeps only the buildings whose bounding box overlaps the ray's own box, so most footprints are rejected with four comparisons and never get a full point-in-polygon test. The verdicts and captions for a batch of results are computed once, off the main thread, before the list updates.

Data size is dealt with when the tiles are built. The live IGN fetches left the app in late September; buildings now come as prebuilt tiles, one per cell of about 200 m. Coordinates are stored at 1e-5° (around a metre), each vertex written as the step from the one before, and the tiles are gzipped. Tiles exist only for cells within 100 m of a bar, café, restaurant or terrace, so the empty parts of a city cost nothing. The phone keeps them cached across launches, so a second evening in the same streets fetches nothing. A cell with fewer than 20 footprints never gets the filter, because an answer there would mean nothing.

Testing, and what it still misses

Each piece has tests on made-up blocks: open ground stays sunny until sunset, a wall to the west cuts the evening short, a wall to the east ends a morning shade, and night stays shaded. Then came live checks. Around the Opéra at 17:30 one September day, 34 of the 219 open-air permits within 400 m were in the sun. In Toulouse, a morning test showed one terrace sunny until just before noon and three others shaded all day in their narrow streets. Every covered city then got at least one checked spot, and a city that read "no sunny terrace" at both 11:00 and 15:00 got a second spot and a second look.

  • Trees, awnings and balconies are in none of the data.
  • A permit is one point for a terrace that can run 10 m along the pavement.
  • The one-metre step could, in principle, skip a very thin building.
  • Where most heights are estimates, the answer describes the typical street, not a measurement of yours.

What the method does reliably is tell the north pavement of a narrow street from an open square, and that is usually the choice you are making. The filter is in Compromise, under Filters.
