How Compromise ranks places by travel time

22 November 2026 · By AlephB · About Compromise

An earlier article, Fair means time, not distance, explains why a fair meeting place is one where everyone's journey takes about the same time. This one covers the machinery: how the app chooses which places to consider, where each person's journey time comes from, how the places are ordered, and what I got wrong along the way. One hard number shapes most of it. Apple Maps answers about fifty directions requests a minute, and every journey time costs one.

Where to look is separate from how to rank

A search asks two questions. Where should we look for places? And once we have places, which is best? Keeping the two apart is the most important rule in the code, and the easiest one to break by accident.

The first question gets a cheap answer. The app measures each person's pace once, with one journey time to the geometric midpoint of the group, and moves the search centre toward whoever is slowest. With three or more people it then takes one half-step toward the person furthest away in time, and keeps that step only if it narrows the gap. The centre decides where to search and never scores a place.

The centre is a straight-line idea, and transit does not run in straight lines. Two friends at either end of one London Underground line were once offered spots 24 minutes from each of them. The middle of the straight line sat off the line, so every journey was the ride plus a ten-minute walk, while pubs by the stations on the line were 12 to 19 minutes each and were never measured. Now, when anyone travels by transit, the app also measures up to six stations between the two people farthest apart, then up to three more near the winner, and searches around the fairest station as well as around the centre. A third search area, added later, looks where the estimate's favourite places cluster inside the box spanned by everyone's starting points. None of these areas ever touches the ranking.

Finding candidates

Places come from two sources. The first is one Apple Maps search that combines a category filter with a search word such as "bar". The category alone was not enough: Apple tags some offices as nightlife, and filtering on the category removes nothing because they all match. The word brings in Apple's relevance ranking, which keeps the offices out. Apple treats the search area as a hint rather than a limit, so the app drops results outside the radius afterwards. The second source is OpenStreetMap venues that Apple lacks. They are read from tiles the app downloads, never waited on for more than two seconds, and added only when no Apple place with a matching name stands within 50 metres.

The radius starts at a quarter of the group's spread, at least 250 metres and at most 1,000. If that ring holds fewer than three places, it doubles, up to four rings, which reaches 2 km from the floor. Only the search repeats. Ranking runs once, on whatever the final ring found.

Where the minutes come from

Every journey time comes from Apple. For each person and each place, the app sends MapKit's ETA request, which returns a single duration, in that person's own mode: walking, cycling, driving or transit. Nothing on the ranking side is computed from average speeds.

That choice has limits. For transit, Apple gives a total and nothing else: no lines, no changes, no route. I first used the full route request for every mode, and transit failed on every single request with an error that looked exactly like "no transit here". The cause was my call: transit is supported only for ETAs. Real gaps exist too. Cape Town, Seoul, Istanbul and Bogotá returned no Apple transit at all when I checked, so the app tells that person transit is not available rather than claiming there is nowhere to go.

A place that one person cannot reach is dropped. Telling "no route" apart from "too many requests" mattered more than I expected, because Apple's throttle sometimes comes back with the same error code as a point off the map. Before I separated the two, a burst of requests read as "no route", emptied the list, and once told a walker that walking was not available where they were.

A budget of about fifty a minute

A measured place costs one request per person. The app keeps a sliding sixty-second ledger of every directions request it has sent and allows itself 48. In an October study, a search with nothing cached cost 6 requests for one person, 14 for two, 27 for three, 36 for four and 45 for five, counting the centre, five candidates and the route lines drawn for the top result. So the app measures at most six places up front, fewer when the last minute's requests are spent, and the rest wait in order to be measured as you scroll. If a search is cut short, the list stays on screen with "Updating in about N s" and the search runs again when the budget allows.

A cache does much of the work. Each answer is stored with its start and end rounded to about 11 metres, the mode, and the hour of departure. Walking and cycling times keep for seven days. Driving and transit times for "now" keep for ten minutes, because of live traffic; for a planned departure they keep up to 72 hours, never past the hour asked. Apple's terms allow map data to be stored only temporarily and only for performance, so the cache stays private to the device and is used for nothing else.

Guessing before measuring

With up to thirty candidates and only six measurements, the order in which places get measured decides a lot. Each candidate gets an estimate: the person's measured time to the midpoint, plus the extra straight-line distance at a fixed marginal pace for their mode. The paces are 1.35 metres a second on foot, 4.5 by bike, 5.5 by transit and 6.5 by car.

My first version multiplied each person's measured pace by the whole distance. A ten-minute transit hop over a kilometre is mostly waiting and walking to the platform, so that version decided the person was slow everywhere, and every pick crept toward their door. The estimate only chooses what to measure and is never shown as a time, and the two places nearest the centre are always measured whatever it says. Since November, a transit rider's estimate also tries a ride on one line, or on two with one change, using line data around their start.

The ranking itself

Ranking is a plain sort over times already measured, with no network, so it can be tested on its own. Settings offers two rules. Shortest trips, the default, puts first the place whose longest journey is shortest, with total time as the tiebreak. Most equal trips puts first the place with the smallest gap between the longest and the shortest journey. For two people they often agree, since the shortest longest trip sits where both times are equal.

Equal trips on its own has a trap. When only exact ties were broken, a place everyone reaches in 40 minutes, a gap of zero, beat one they reach in 10 and 12. The developer tool still uses a five-minute band: every place within five minutes of the best gap counts as equally fair, and among those the lowest total wins.

Two more rules apply after ranking and cost no requests. A trip limit drops any place where someone would travel longer than the cap. And for a pair, each person's direct trip to the other's starting point is measured; a place that either of them reaches no sooner than that trip is dropped, unless dropping it would empty the list.

Departure time

Times are asked for the departure you choose, either leaving at a time or arriving by one. The hour is part of the cache key, so Friday at seven and Monday at noon are different journeys. Opening hours, where known, are read at each person's arrival time rather than at the departure.

The hour can change the result. One test case of two transit riders gave a different best place for "now" than for the next Thursday evening, and my test bench had to pin its departures to a fixed date, because a rerun later the same day was asking different questions.

Checking against a better answer

Until November I checked search quality by hand. Then I built a bench: 28 fixed cases across eight cities, with two to four people and every mode. For each case it gathers a much wider pool of places, orders them with the app's own estimate, and measures the top 40 (30 for three or more people) with real directions, slowly, caching every result. The best of those is the reference, and the app's top pick is scored by how many minutes worse it is.

The first round was humbling. The reference place was on the app's first page in 5 cases of 28, and not among its candidates at all in 21. In 16 of those 21 the estimate ranked it in its top ten, so the search area had lost them and the ordering was fine. Measuring stations on every transit search, choosing OpenStreetMap's places by estimate rather than by distance, and adding the clustered search area took the median extra minutes on the longest trip from 2.7 to 0.2. The cost was a median of 30 requests per search instead of 16, and a first page that took about 8 seconds instead of 3.8 in a debug build. Profiling showed that about half of that time was my own code on the critical path, not Apple. After fixes the final page was back to 3.8 seconds at the median, with the same top pick in 23 of 28 cases.

I also compared Apple's transit times with Google's on 50 journeys in two cities. There was no constant offset, but the difference between two people's errors in one case had a median of 3.9 minutes in one city and 3.2 in the other. That is as large as the margins the top places are ranked on, and 5 of 10 cases changed their top pick on Google's times. Places within three or four minutes of each other on transit are equally fair within the data's error.

Lessons

  • Keep where to look and how to rank apart. Every shortcut that mixed them made the results worse.
  • A throttled search looks exactly like an empty one. Report it as a wait.
  • Check the call before blaming the coverage. Transit was there all along; I used the wrong request.
  • An estimate is an ordering. It chooses what to measure and is never shown.
  • Test against a better answer you can afford to compute slowly. Most of my misses were places the app never considered.

If you want to see these rules at work with your own friends, Compromise is on the iPhone.
